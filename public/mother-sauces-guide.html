The Five
Mother Sauces

Every classical French sauce traces back to one of five bases. Learn the mother, and the daughters come almost for free. Tap a sauce to open it up.

1833
Carême codifies four: béchamel, velouté, espagnole, allemande
1903–07
Escoffier demotes allemande to a velouté derivative, adds tomate + hollandaise
The Five
Béchamel sauce blanche
white roux + milk
+
Roux ratio1:1 butter : flour
DifficultyEasiest of the five
Time~15 min
Technique
  1. Melt butter, whisk in equal parts flour, cook 1–2 min without coloring — this is a white roux.
  2. Gradually whisk in warm milk to avoid lumps.
  3. Simmer 10 min, stirring often.
  4. Season with salt, white pepper, and a pinch of nutmeg. Add an onion piqué (onion studded with a clove, tucked in with a bay leaf) while it simmers, then remove before serving.
Common Derivatives
Mornay — + gruyère & parmesan Soubise — + onion purée Cream Sauce — + extra cream
Uncommon Derivatives
Nantua — + crayfish butter, cream Mustard Sauce — + dijon

Velouté sauce blonde
blonde roux + white stock
+
Roux ratio1:1 butter : flour
StockChicken, veal, or fish
Time~30 min
Technique
  1. Cook the roux slightly longer than béchamel's, to a pale gold ("blonde") stage.
  2. Gradually whisk in warm white stock — match the stock to the dish (chicken for poultry, fish for seafood).
  3. Simmer 20–30 min, skimming impurities that rise to the surface.
  4. Strain for a silkier finish.
Common Derivatives
Suprême — + cream, mushroom Allemande — + egg yolk, cream, lemon Bercy — + white wine, shallots
Uncommon Derivatives
Normande — + cream, mushroom essence Hongroise — + paprika, onion
Trivia

Allemande used to be one of Carême's original four mother sauces. Escoffier later reclassified it as a velouté derivative — a demotion, technically, but it still shows up constantly.

Espagnole sauce brune
brown roux + brown stock + tomato
+
RouxCooked deep brown, nutty
StockVeal or beef
Time1+ hour
Technique
  1. Cook the roux to a deep brown — right up to the edge of burning, without crossing it. This is the "Sunday project" sauce.
  2. Whisk in brown stock gradually along with a mirepoix (diced onion, carrot, celery) and tomato purée.
  3. Simmer at least an hour, skimming frequently.
  4. Strain for a clean, glossy sauce.
Common Derivatives
Demi-Glace — 1:1 with stock, reduced, splash of sherry Bordelaise — + red wine, shallots, bone marrow Chasseur — + mushrooms, shallots, white wine
Uncommon Derivatives
Robert — + onion, white wine, mustard Charcutière — Robert + gherkins Lyonnaise — + onions, vinegar reduction

Tomate sauce tomate
roux (or reduction) + tomato
+
Two pathsRoux-based or roux-free
Time30–45 min
Technique
  1. Classic French route: light roux, tomatoes, mirepoix, and a bit of pork or bacon for richness, simmered until thick.
  2. Italian route (more common today): skip the roux entirely — just reduce tomatoes with aromatics over medium-low heat until thick and concentrated.
  3. Season with salt, a touch of sugar to balance acidity, and finish with olive oil or butter off heat.
Common Derivatives
Provençale — + garlic, herbs, olive oil Creole — + peppers, onion, celery Portugaise — + onion, garlic, tomato concassé
Uncommon Derivatives
Spanish Sauce — + peppers, ham

Hollandaise sauce hollandaise
egg yolk + clarified butter + acid
+
Yolk capacity~8 oz butter / yolk max
Ideal yolk temp150–155°F
Failure riskBreaks easiest of the five
Technique
  1. Whisk yolks + 1 tbsp cold water over a double boiler (barely simmering, bowl not touching water) until pale, thick, and ribbon-y — the sabayon stage.
  2. Remove from heat. Add melted, slightly cooled butter drop by drop, whisking fully between each addition, until the emulsion visibly "grabs."
  3. Once it's holding, switch to a thin, steady stream, whisking continuously until all butter is in.
  4. Finish with lemon juice, salt, and a pinch of cayenne.
Common Derivatives
Béarnaise — tarragon-shallot-vinegar reduction instead of lemon Mousseline — + whipped cream, folded in Maltaise — + blood orange juice & zest
Uncommon Derivatives
Choron — béarnaise + tomato paste Noisette — made with brown butter (beurre noisette) Paloise — béarnaise, mint instead of tarragon Moutarde — + dijon mustard

Knife Cuts

The cuts below are drawn to true relative scale — each tile represents the same 20 mm square, so you can see how much finer a brunoise really is than a mirepoix. Uniform size isn't fussiness: recipe timings assume it, and mismatched pieces cook at different rates.

Brunoise
2 mm cubes
The finest dice, cut from julienne. Garnish work and refined sauces — where you want the vegetable present but nearly invisible.
Macédoine
5 mm cubes
Small dice you actually eat as part of the dish rather than strain out. Vegetable salads, garnishes with texture.
Julienne
2 mm × ~40 mm matchsticks
Long thin strips. Squaring off the vegetable first is what makes them uniform. Garnish, quick-cooking preparations.
Jardinière
3 mm × 25 mm batons
Short batons sized to sit neatly on a soup spoon — the reason it exists.
Mirepoix
Rough ~1–2 cm chunks
Deliberately coarse. It flavors espagnole and stocks and then gets strained out, so precision would be wasted effort here.

All tiles = 20 mm × 20 mm at identical scale

Practice order
Square off the vegetable → slice into even planks → stack and cut into batons → rotate a quarter turn and dice. Julienne and brunoise are the same motion, just stopped at different points. Master that one sequence and four of these five come with it.

Technique Glossary

ROUX — fat + flour cooked together, the thickening base for four of the five mothers. Color (white → blonde → brown) depends on cook time and determines flavor and thickening power.
EMULSION — fat dispersed into tiny droplets suspended in a liquid, held together by egg yolk lecithin. Hollandaise's method, not a roux.
REDUCTION — simmering a liquid uncovered to evaporate water and concentrate flavor — how demi-glace and béarnaise's vinegar base are built.
MIREPOIX — diced onion, carrot, and celery — the aromatic backbone under espagnole and tomate.
ONION PIQUÉ — an onion studded with a clove and tucked with a bay leaf, simmered in béchamel for background flavor, then discarded.
